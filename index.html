Case 01

Addressing bottlenecks

Current role
MH3D · Medical-device hardware · Process & operations
At a medical-device startup, I’m turning manual, one-person tasks into tools anyone can run, and fixing how the team stores what it knows.
Context

I joined MH3D, a medical-device hardware company, as a technical project manager. The first task I took ownership of was a fully manual one: running a geometric calibration on every semiconductor to catch its individual defects. Learning about the process took longer than automating it would have and occupied both me and the person that built it.

The pattern

The same problem ran through most of the company’s software: manual, undocumented, and known to one engineer each. On-site installs at client sites required frequent calls back to the engineering team. It was slow and had no backup. If the owner was out sick, the work stopped.

Automating the bottlenecks

I had the team list every process like this and rank it by how much it cost us. Now I work through them in that order, turning one-person tasks into tools anyone can run. That saves time, and it means no single absence can stop a delivery or a client install.

Making knowledge visible

The product was also hard to learn, since the documentation was scattered across tools. I set about giving greater visibility to the team’s work and thought process. I had done this before at a seed-stage startup, where redesigning our documentation process had paid off in clear ways. There, onboarding dropped from two weeks to three days. The team stopped losing about 30 minutes a week to searching Google Docs on calls. A new feature took one requirements meeting instead of three, because the design was written down clearly. Decisions got settled once, with everyone in the room, instead of across a run of one-on-ones. I’m setting up the same system at MH3D, where the bar is higher: our documentation has to meet FDA approval as we move beyond pre-clinical work.

Case 02

Running the booth and the build at the same time

Yield! (Billionworlds) · PAX, 2023 · Event operations + live product research
When a partner’s delivery fell through two days before a launch event, I kept it on track. When the players who showed up to the booth surprised us with their preferences and demographics, I asked the team to help me A/B test builds during the event.
Act 1

Sourcing the stand

Context

We brought Yield! to PAX with a playable demo. Our publisher was producing the booth’s printed materials (posters, banners, standees) through its own vendor.

The call

Two days before the show, the publisher told us their vendor would not make it in time. They were looking for other options but could not promise anything. The show date was fixed, and an empty booth is not something you fix later. Rather than wait on a maybe that was not even ours to fix, I took it on as our problem to solve.

What I did

That morning I gathered the exact print specs from our team and the publisher, so the order would be right the first time. I found a local print shop, agreed a rush turnaround within our budget, and booked a courier to get everything to the floor in time for setup.

The result

The booth was ready before doors opened, on budget. Nobody on the floor would have guessed the original plan had fallen apart two days earlier.

Act 2

Reading the room, then rebuilding it

Context

We didn’t go to PAX only to demo. We wanted to test whether our player personas matched the people actually drawn to the game. A show floor is a rare chance to watch hundreds of players react in person.

What we found

The audience was more varied than we had assumed. About 15% were couples looking for a co-op game, and they played longer than solo visitors. Among solo players, roughly 35% were women, most of them after a relaxed building game, while many of the men wanted deep, hardcore strategy. Three different reasons to play one game. We could also see exactly where each group gave up.

What I did

Instead of taking those problems home in a report, I asked the team to build a new version overnight. Some were quick changes. Some were revamps of systems we had scrapped. We also built a different control scheme and a new UI layout. Over the next two days I ran the versions as live A/B tests on the floor and noted which persona each tester fit, so we could see what worked for which kind of player.

The result

Average session length rose 20%. Players got past several of the points where day-one visitors had quit. The controls rating went from 6/10 to 9/10, which told us the new control scheme and UI worked.

Case 03

Reading the data, and telling the community

Barotrauma · Daedalic (publisher) · 2020–2023 · Release ops · Telemetry · Comms
Kept a fast-moving product shippable through two years of constant updates, and used the player data I owned to help steer it toward a solid 1.0.
Context

By its second anniversary, Barotrauma had sold over 800,000 copies, gathered thousands of reviews, and grown a Discord of tens of thousands. It had been a while since the question changed from whether the game worked to how to make it sell more. On the publisher side we had raised our investment to match, funding a bigger dev team and a wider scope. I was part of that decision.

The problem

The success brought its own problem. Two years of fast output from the studio, around 29,000 commits and thousands of tickets, plus dozens of new creatures, missions, submarines, dungeons, and big campaign and UI overhauls, left the game with far more systems than planned and a lot of technical debt. To deliver a complete, finished product rather than a longer feature list, the roadmap had to shift from adding more content to polishing, balancing, and tying the existing systems together.

What I did

I owned the data strategy and community communications for that pivot. I instrumented telemetry and built Power BI dashboards that gave a cross-functional dev team with limited analytics maturity visibility into actual player behavior: which systems they never touched, where they quit, what they skipped. I facilitated prioritization sessions to evaluate trade-offs, and we aligned on a roadmap that deprioritized new content in favor of integrating existing systems to deepen engagement. I then drove communication to a community of tens of thousands, framing the shift in cadence toward polish and fixes around player value. The audience stayed with us through the change.

On the release side

Through all of it, I kept the game shippable across two years of constant early-access builds. That meant certification on six platforms and across rating boards (ESRB, PEGI, USK), localization in over 10 languages, and store listings and assets. None of the fast output ever missed a release.

The result

Barotrauma reached 1.0 in March 2023 as a finished product, with onboarding, faction progression, and a real campaign ending chosen over yet more content. Every platform and ratings deadline was met across the whole early-access run.

Case 04

Building an esports league, and the rule that held twice

Civ VI World Cup · Co-founder & organizer · 2019–present
Brought five separate communities into one international league. When a cheating case hit, I turned a hard judgment call into a rule that held up when the same thing happened again.
Context

Four national communities (French, Russian, Korean, and Brazilian), along the english-speaking Civ Players League had been running on their own, and I brought them into one league. I helped write the early rulebook, set the format and bracket, ran one of the first teams, and recruited others community by community. Later I brought in casters to broadcast matches and worked with content creators to grow the scene.

The problem

Mid-tournament, one player was caught watching a caster’s live broadcast of his own match, which could show him his opponents’ positions. He said it was an accident, caused by twitch’s raid feature, and we couldn’t prove otherwise. The case pointed at a deeper problem. The whole tournament ran on an honor system. There were ways to cheat we couldn’t detect, and no money to pay for real enforcement, so it worked on trust and reputation alone. The decision came down to a bigger question: what do we do the first time that honor system is tested and we can’t settle the facts?

The call

Our organizing team leaned toward letting it go. Several captains pushed back hard. I made a call, and based it on what actually mattered: with no prize money, the thing worth protecting was whether teams still wanted to keep playing. So rather than hand down a verdict, I asked every captain one question: if this were your player, how would you want it handled? They set the standard together and I brought the organizing team onboard. We added a rule mid-season: the team was banned for that season and welcome back the next. Then I spoke with each player on the banned team myself and took them through how we got there and why.

The result

The team was banned, and we stayed on good terms. I’m still close with two of its players years later, and I see one of them whenever I’m in New York, which tells me the decision read as fair rather than personal. The rule proved itself two years later, when almost the same case came up with a different team. The first time it had been a drama. The second time it was routine, because the rule already existed and everyone knew the reasoning behind it.
